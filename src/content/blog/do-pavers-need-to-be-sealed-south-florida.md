Sealing is one of the first questions homeowners ask after a new driveway or pool deck goes in. The short answer is no, pavers do not need to be sealed to function. A well-built paver surface will last for decades whether you seal it or not. Sealing is a choice about how the surface looks and how much upkeep you want to do, not a requirement for it to hold together. Here is what sealing actually does, when it is worth it, and when you can skip it.

## What sealing actually does

A sealer is a liquid coating that soaks into the paver and the joint sand between them. Once it cures, it does a few practical things:

- It slows down staining. Oil, rust, leaf tannins, and barbecue grease sit on the surface longer instead of soaking straight in, so you have time to clean them up.
- It locks the joint sand in place. The sand between pavers is what keeps them from shifting, and a sealer helps hold it against wind, rain, and pressure washing.
- It deepens the color. Most sealers bring out the natural tone of the stone, and some add a light sheen if you want one.
- It makes routine cleaning easier, because dirt has less to grip.

What sealing does not do is hold the pavers together or fix a bad installation. The strength comes from the base and the joint sand, not the coating on top.

## The case for sealing

In South Florida, the reasons to seal are mostly about sun and water. Strong UV fades color over the years, and a sealer with UV protection slows that. Frequent rain and pool splash-out wash joint sand out of the gaps, and sealed joints hold up better. If you have a light travertine pool deck or a warm-toned driveway you picked specifically for its color, sealing helps keep that color looking new for longer.

Sealing also helps in high-stain spots. A driveway that catches engine drips, or a patio under a mango tree, stays cleaner and wipes up easier when it is sealed.

## The case for skipping it

Sealing is not free and it is not permanent. A few honest downsides:

- It wears off. A sealer lasts roughly two to five years depending on the product and the traffic, so it is a repeating cost, not a one-time job.
- The wrong sealer, or sealing too soon, can trap moisture and cause a cloudy white haze. New pavers usually need to cure and fully dry out first, often several weeks to a couple of months.
- A glossy finish can get slippery when wet, which matters around a pool. Matte penetrating sealers avoid this.

If you like the natural, matte look of unsealed stone, and you are fine cleaning the surface once or twice a year, leaving pavers bare is a perfectly valid choice. Plenty of beautiful South Florida patios are never sealed at all.

## The two types of sealer

Most products fall into one of two families, and the difference matters.

**Penetrating sealers** soak into the paver and protect from within. They leave the surface looking natural and matte, they do not get slippery, and they let the stone breathe. This is usually the safer pick for pool decks and for natural stone like travertine.

**Film-forming (topical) sealers** sit on top and leave a visible finish, from a soft satin to a wet look. They give the richest color boost and the most stain resistance, but they can get slick when wet and they show wear over time. They suit driveways and patios more than pool surrounds.

## When to seal, and how often

Timing is the part people get wrong most often. A few simple rules:

- On new pavers, wait until they have fully cured and dried. Sealing too early is the usual cause of that milky haze. When in doubt, wait longer.
- Always clean and let the surface dry completely first. Sealing over dirt or damp sand locks the problem in.
- Reseal every two to five years, or when water stops beading and the color starts to look flat.

## Does sealing change how long pavers last?

Not really. The lifespan of a paver surface comes from the base, the edge restraint, and the joints, which is the same set of things we cover in [how long pavers last](/blog/how-long-do-pavers-last-south-florida/). Sealing protects appearance and makes maintenance easier. It is a cosmetic and convenience upgrade, not a structural one. A sealed surface and an unsealed one, both built well, will both be here in thirty years.

## The bottom line

Pavers do not need to be sealed. They need a solid base and full joints, and those are set the day they go in. Seal them if you want richer color, easier cleaning, and better protection against sun and stains, and you do not mind redoing it every few years. Skip it if you prefer the natural matte look and a simpler routine. Either way is fine.
