## Identify the cover before designing around it

Make a simple cover inventory from normal ground-level observation. Photograph the closed cover and a wider view showing its position relative to the driveway edge, wall or planting. Note visible markings without disturbing it, plus cracks, a damaged edge or a surface that appears higher or lower than neighboring pavers. Record observations, not diagnoses.

Then establish who can identify the equipment and confirm ownership. An old household note or an installer's guess is a starting point for questions, not confirmation. A cover near the property boundary does not tell you who may change it. Leave covers closed and have identification or assessment that requires access handled by the responsible professional.

Keep each cover separate in your notes. If its owner or purpose remains unknown, mark that decision as unresolved rather than letting a driveway drawing assign it an assumed function. Share previous service records if you have them, without treating a past repair as approval for the replacement plan.

## Confirm vehicle use and finished level together

A small plastic lid should not be assumed suitable for vehicles because cars have passed nearby for years. Describe how your household actually uses the driveway: parking, reversing, turning near the edge and occasional service access. Give that information to the utility owner or qualified professional evaluating the cover, its supporting parts and any proposed replacement.

Ask for the exact component identification and documentation supporting the proposed use. If replacement is suggested, confirm who selects and approves the parts. A matching color or familiar shape is not enough to establish that a substitute belongs on the existing equipment.

The proposed driveway finish also needs coordination. Have the professionals resolve the relationship between the cover, its frame and surrounding pavers before you approve the layout. Do not assume that raising or lowering nearby pavers authorizes an adjustment to utility equipment, or that the installer can relocate a cover to improve symmetry.

## Visible access is only part of excavation planning

A known cover does not identify every buried facility or its route. Coordinate the required locating before digging. Sunshine 811's [Homeowner: Every Dig Calls for 811](https://sunshine811.com/homeowner-lp) explains that it notifies member utilities; those utilities or their locators mark facilities. Sunshine 811 itself does not locate them. Put responsibility for that coordination in the project brief.

Keep the cover inventory and excavation planning together, but recognize their different purposes. Photographs help the team discuss visible equipment; they are not a subsurface survey. Avoid letting a clean driveway sketch create confidence about conditions nobody has verified.

## Put the division of work in writing

The proposal should say who coordinates the work and who performs it. Those can be different parties. A driveway contractor can identify a dependency in the schedule without undertaking utility work, and a homeowner should not have to infer responsibility from a general promise to complete the driveway.

Use the written scope to settle these questions:

- **Coordination:** Who contacts the equipment owner, collects its response and tells the driveway installer when decisions are ready?
- **Level adjustment:** Who assesses whether adjustment is needed, who is authorized to perform it, and what must happen before surrounding pavers are installed?
- **Product approval:** Who confirms the existing or replacement cover and associated parts are suitable for the proposed use?
- **Surrounding pavers:** Who removes and restores affected units, and does that work fall within the driveway proposal or a separate agreement?
- **Changes:** Who documents an unexpected condition and obtains your agreement before additional work proceeds?

## Review appearance after access is resolved

Once the equipment decisions are confirmed, review the pattern where it meets each cover. Ask the installer to show the intended surrounding cuts and explain how the layout preserves the access confirmed by the responsible professional. A drawing can help you judge appearance, but a paver grid is not an engineering plan or approval to modify equipment.

Consider the view from the street and from the usual walking approach. A deliberately arranged border may be more acceptable than a layout that tries to hide a cover. Make the aesthetic choice with the actual access conditions understood, rather than asking for concealment and discovering the conflict after installation.

For interlocking concrete pavers, CMHA's [Operation and Maintenance Guide for Interlocking Concrete Pavement](https://www.cmha.org/resource/pav-tec-006/) discusses assessing condition and distress and removing and reinstating individual units for repair. It does not establish utility ownership or a cover's rating. Its concrete-system repair guidance should not be transferred to porcelain.

Ask how future equipment service might affect the surrounding surface and who you would contact for restoration. Keep the selected paver identification and approved layout with your household records. Discuss spare material with the installer; matching a later repair should be a planning question, not an assumed outcome.

## Keep access in the work sequence

Before materials arrive, have the team explain how equipment access will remain available through removal, preparation and installation. This gives you a practical basis for discussing the schedule without assuming every trade can arrive together.
