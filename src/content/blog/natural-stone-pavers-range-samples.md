## Start with a product you can actually order

Give each candidate a clear identity: supplier, product name or code, finish, dimensions, and thickness. Find out whether the display pieces come from the supply being quoted, an earlier shipment, or a general showroom collection. Record any available lot identification. A sample that cannot be connected to an order is an inspiration reference, not a settled selection.

The Natural Stone Institute's [Travertine Selection: Good, Better, Best](https://www.naturalstoneinstitute.org/default/assets/file/travertine-good,%20better,%20best-handout%202019.pdf) recommends requesting a range of samples and notes that variation exists even within one quarry.

Have the supplier explain what the group represents. Does it include the lightest and darkest appearances expected in the proposed supply, or only the most consistent pieces? If current stock is unavailable for review, identify what can be confirmed now and what requires another approval. Keep that open question visible before committing to an order.

Also ask whether the arrangement reflects the expected balance of tones or merely shows the outer limits. A group assembled to display every extreme is not necessarily the blend you are ordering. If the supplier cannot describe that balance, record the uncertainty rather than treating a pleasing arrangement as a delivery promise.

Request pieces large enough to judge the proposed format. If several sizes are included, see examples of those sizes together. Clarify whether borders or other adjoining stone pieces are supplied under the same description or require their own selection. Do not assume the display answers those questions.

## Review the group where you will see it

Set the samples beside the house finish and, where relevant, the existing pool finish. Review them in the area's ordinary sun and shade. Use the actual surroundings rather than relying entirely on a showroom photograph. This is an appearance exercise, not a test of surface temperature or slip performance.

Look from the doorway, then from the place you expect to sit. Move back far enough to see the group as part of the garden. A piece you admire close up may draw more attention than you want when repeated across the view. Decide whether the overall impression supports the room you are creating outside.

For a project involving [patio pavers installation in West Palm Beach](/services/patios/west-palm-beach/), include the patio's outline and adjoining surfaces in that review. If the new area meets an older walkway, place samples near that meeting point. Decide whether you want a deliberate contrast or a close visual relationship, then discuss whether the offered selection can achieve it.

If another decision maker shares the home, review the same group together. Record disagreements while alternatives are still available. A favorite sample chosen separately by each person can leave the contractor with incompatible expectations.

## Define an acceptable appearance before ordering

Describe what you like in terms someone else can recognize. Note the preferred overall tone, the contrast between neighboring pieces, and whether pronounced markings should remain prominent in the layout. Include photographs showing the whole group, not just the piece you like most. Mark which examples you accept and which need discussion.

Imagine the sample with the boldest markings near the doorway rather than at the garden edge. Would you still approve it? If acceptance depends on hiding certain pieces, tell the supplier and installer before ordering. Ask whether sorting or a particular placement approach is feasible, included in the proposal, and compatible with the material available.

Distinguish a preference from a condition of approval. You might prefer a quiet overall appearance but require another review if the proposed supply changes. Put that requirement in writing. Avoid an approval note that merely says beige stone, since it leaves the finish, size, reference pieces, and unresolved decisions unidentified.

An approved sample group does not promise identical stones throughout the project. Its purpose is to give you and the supplier a shared reference. If nobody can explain what a disputed piece would be compared against, the approval record needs more work.

## Confirm suitability separately from beauty

The Natural Stone Institute's [Travertine, Limestone, and Dolomitic Limestone](https://www.naturalstoneinstitute.org/consumers/varieties/limestone/) describes differences among these varieties and distinguishes exterior wall cladding from horizontal exterior use. A stone's history on a building facade does not establish suitability for an outdoor walking surface.

Have the supplier identify the exact exterior product and intended application. Have the installer or an appropriate stone professional evaluate that product, its finish and dimensions, and the proposed installation for your site. Keep technical questions open until the appropriate person answers them; approving a color does not answer them.

If the candidate is travertine and its face treatment is still undecided, review the separate guide to [filled and unfilled travertine pavers](/blog/travertine-pavers-filled-unfilled-voids/). Settle that product choice before treating its sample group as final.

## Assign materials and quantities to each area

The written scope should connect the approved selection to specific areas. Identify the main patio surface, any border, and adjoining work separately where their materials differ. If existing pavers will be reused, show where they will go and which areas receive newly ordered material.

A verified Boynton Beach written project scope separates pool-deck work from a front walkway using pavers salvaged from the pool deck. It illustrates why material allocation belongs in writing. It does not document stone sample approval or a completed West Palm Beach installation.

Ask for the quantity calculation behind the order, including allowances for cuts and the spare pieces you intend to retain. Have the contractor explain those allowances for your layout rather than inserting a generic percentage. Confirm who owns the spares, where they will be stored, and how they will be identified for future reference.

Make substitutions an explicit decision. A proposed change in product, finish, dimensions, or supply should state what needs renewed approval and whether it changes cost or scheduling. Resolve the approval process before an unavailable item becomes an urgent purchasing choice.

## Preserve the reference and plan the delivery review

Keep the approved physical reference group available through delivery and installation. Attach its dated photographs, product identifiers, accepted appearance notes, and supplier correspondence to the selection record. Name who holds the pieces and make sure the installer can access them. Photos support that record; they should not replace the physical reference.

Agree who will compare delivered material with the order and reference, and when that review happens before installation. If the lot differs or the appearance raises questions, the written process should identify whom to contact, how to document the concern, and who authorizes the next step. Clarify return or replacement terms before ordering. Natural variation does not excuse damaged pieces or delivery of the wrong product.
