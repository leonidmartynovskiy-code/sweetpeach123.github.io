Before approving paver cleaning, agree on a written protection and containment plan for the surrounding property. Identify the glass, door tracks, metal, painted finishes, stone, plants and pool features that are outside the cleaning scope but could be affected by the work. The plan should record their condition, assign protection and removal responsibilities, and explain how the contractor will assess runoff and access. Leave unresolved surfaces or exposure routes on hold until the contractor can explain how they will be managed.

## Walk the surroundings before comparing proposals

Use an overview photograph to mark the cleaning boundary and everything immediately beside it. Include the route the crew will use to reach the pavers. A doorway outside the treatment area may still sit on that route.

Give nearby features recognizable names:

- Glass and doors: sliding panels, frames, seals, tracks and the threshold at the living room entrance.
- Metal and paint: railings, hardware, painted trim, walls and decorative finishes beside the work.
- Stone: thresholds, wall bases, steps or borders that will remain untreated.
- Landscaping: planted beds, overhanging foliage, irrigation components and freestanding planters.
- Pool surroundings: coping, the water edge, equipment access and features retained outside the cleaning scope.

These names let you discuss a specific location when the proposal says protection is included. If stone itself is being considered for treatment, our guide to [cleaning stone beside concrete pavers](/blog/cleaning-stone-and-concrete-pavers/) covers that separate decision. Here, the question is what happens around the approved cleaning area.

## Record the condition you are starting with

Photograph adjacent surfaces with the contractor before work begins. Pair a wider view showing location with closer views of existing scratches, chips, discoloration, worn paint or damaged foliage. Note door operation concerns and places where water has previously collected or entered. Record observations without guessing their cause.

Keep the original files and share an agreed set with the person supervising the work. A picture of an unidentified scratch is difficult to compare later; a location label makes it useful. Include known recent painting, glass treatments, stone repairs or landscape work in the discussion. Missing information should remain marked as unknown, with responsibility assigned for resolving it before that area is exposed.

## Ask how the contractor will check compatibility

Request the proposed product names and ask how the contractor will check them against neighboring surfaces and finishes. A description such as professional cleaning does not answer whether the plan accounts for your coated frame, painted trim or stone threshold. Ask which checks remain outstanding before approval.

CMHA's [Cleaning, Sealing and Joint Sand Stabilization of Interlocking Concrete Pavement](https://www.cmha.org/resource/pav-tec-005/) addresses concrete pavers. It emphasizes protecting vegetation, considering cleaner compatibility and runoff direction, following product instructions and using experienced professionals. It does not provide a protection specification for every neighboring finish.

The Natural Stone Institute's [Care & Cleaning of Natural Stone](https://www.naturalstoneinstitute.org/consumers/care/) explains that care depends on stone composition and acids can etch or dull calcareous stone. That supports a separate compatibility check for a neighboring stone threshold; it does not make a concrete-paver treatment suitable there.

Temporary protection needs its own agreed scope. Ask what the contractor proposes, who installs and checks it, and who removes it. Plant covering is not automatically safe for an extended period. Require a plan for monitoring it and responding to delays, including who takes responsibility if the crew leaves. For movable planters, agree whether relocation is appropriate and who handles the move and return.

## Coordinate water, irrigation and access

Have the contractor assess runoff routing on the actual property and explain the proposed containment approach. Identify nearby doors, planting beds, pool edges, drains and low areas in that discussion. An uncertain destination for cleaning water is a question to resolve before authorizing work; the homeowner should not have to design the solution.

Include the irrigation schedule and name whoever controls it. Agree who coordinates any changes, communicates with the landscape provider and confirms the agreed settings afterward. Where a pool professional needs to review a concern, record who arranges that review and what cleaning work depends on it.

Set access boundaries for residents, pets, visitors and other trades. Identify usable entrances and any restricted route, plus the person who can confirm when access changes. Protection that needs to be moved for a delivery or household access should trigger a discussion with the supervisor.

## Why named boundaries matter in a written scope

A written scope for a Boynton Beach project names owner-supplied porcelain pavers for the back pool deck. A separate change order adds a side walkway and garbage-can area. ## What belongs in the estimate

Compare proposals against the same marked photographs and these practical questions:

- **Boundaries:** Which pavers will be cleaned, and which neighboring surfaces must remain untreated? Does the written scope identify both?
- **Condition record:** Which photographs and observations form the starting record, and who acknowledges unresolved concerns?
- **Protection duties:** Who provides, checks, removes and collects temporary protection? Who handles movable items and their return?
- **Water and access:** Has the contractor assessed containment and runoff routing? Who coordinates irrigation, restricted entrances and any pool-related review?
- **Unknowns and changes:** Which items need further assessment, what work stays on hold, and who approves a changed scope or additional charge?
- **Completion:** Who reviews the adjacent surroundings with you, records concerns and remains the contact afterward?

Ask whether protection work is included in the quoted scope or requires a separate item. A proposal that leaves planter handling, a specialist review or removal of temporary protection unresolved is harder to compare with one that assigns those duties. Resolve these differences before choosing on the total alone. Additional assessment may also change the sequence of work; ask which decisions must be settled before a start is confirmed.

## Agree on a discovery pause

Name a supervising contact and a backup, and agree how you will reach them while work is underway. Establish that an unidentified finish, newly noticed damage, displaced protection or water moving beyond the planned boundary prompts a pause in the affected work and a review.

The next step should be documented: the observation, the proposed response and any change requiring your approval. Agree who can authorize resuming that work. This is especially useful when you are away from the property; a photo sent without a clear question should not be treated as permission to expand the job.

## Review more than the cleaned pavers

At the final walkthrough, return to the original surroundings record. Review glass, frames, door tracks, paint, stone, plants and any pool-side boundaries, alongside the cleaned surface. Confirm the agreed handling of temporary protection, moved planters, irrigation settings and access restrictions. Record concerns with their location and the person responsible for follow-up.

Keep the approved plan, changes and completion notes together. If something needs another visit or specialist assessment, ask for that next step in writing before closing the discussion.
