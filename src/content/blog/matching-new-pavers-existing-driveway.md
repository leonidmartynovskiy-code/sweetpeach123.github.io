To match new pavers to an existing driveway, identify the installed units and compare physical samples against a prepared area of the driveway before ordering. Check dimensions, thickness, edge profile and texture alongside color. If an exact blend is unavailable, agree on acceptable contrast, a deliberate border or a defined section of replacement. Have a contractor confirm suitability for driveway use and the existing installation. For a patio addition, also ask how the join will meet the house and drain.

## Identify the installed units before shopping

Gather an old invoice, manufacturer label, delivery paperwork or an unused spare block. Ask the supplier whether these establish the product more reliably than your photographs. If the product cannot be identified confidently, record that uncertainty instead of approving an assumed match.

Ask the contractor and supplier to check:

- **Product identity:** manufacturer, range, shape and color blend, where records establish them.
- **Physical fit:** actual length, width and thickness, plus the edge profile and surface texture.
- **Pattern compatibility:** whether the available shapes work with the existing layout and its visible joints.
- **Current supply:** whether the identified product is available in the needed quantity, and what the supplied sample represents.

When a supplier offers an alternative, ask what differs from the original unit instead of accepting "closest available" as a specification. For a blended product, review a representative selection from the available material. Ask whether the samples show the full variation in the proposed order before approving the range that could arrive.

Ask the contractor to confirm suitable dimensions and vehicle use separately from color approval. What loads will the installation carry, and have the existing levels and base conditions been assessed? Request that assessment before deciding whether the proposed units and scope are feasible.

Ask what caused the movement and whether the proposed work addresses it.

## Compare samples against a fair reference

Bring physical samples to the property and view them beside the area that will remain. Compare dry with dry and wet with wet; look in sun and shade when those conditions occur. Record which condition informed your decision instead of approving whichever photograph looks closest. Ask the supplier what uncertainty remains if only a photograph is available.

First agree how the existing comparison area will be prepared. In its [Cleaning, Sealing and Joint Sand Stabilization of Interlocking Concrete Pavement](https://www.cmha.org/resource/pav-tec-005/), CMHA explains that strong-acid cleaners can change color and recommends testing a small area first. It also describes differences in sealer appearance. Ask whether cleaning or a proposed finish would change the reference you are using to approve the new material.

Keep paver cleaning and sealing separate from material supply and installation in the proposal. Decide whether maintenance is needed to establish a fair reference, is part of the finished work, or is outside this project. If a finish is proposed, ask to evaluate its appearance on both the retained and proposed material before committing. Record the visible difference you accept after that comparison.

## Matching new pavers to an existing patio

For a patio addition or extension, approve a sample layout where the old surface will meet the new work. Ask whether the proposed units fit the existing pattern and what the join will look like from the house. Agree on the retained area before ordering material for the extension.

Would it sit across the view from your main door, at the edge of the existing patio, or within an area you already plan to replace? Ask to see the transition with enough sample units to judge the pattern. Include proposed cut pieces in that review.

At the house, ask what finished levels are proposed beside door thresholds and how the new work will meet the wall. Will any existing step or threshold detail need attention? If the addition reaches a pool deck or screen enclosure, who will check that edge and identify what must remain undisturbed? Ask whether the scope affects the enclosure supports or an existing drain before approving the boundary.

## Define the variation you can accept

CMHA's [Operation and Maintenance Guide for Interlocking Concrete Pavement](https://www.cmha.org/resource/pav-tec-006/) explains that weathering, wear and stains can make installed units look different from stored spares. It recommends retaining undamaged units and distributing replacements among existing units to reduce obvious variation. Discuss whether that approach is appropriate for your scope; it does not establish what you personally must accept.

Judge the driveway comparison from the street and your usual walking route, then inspect it at close range. Which differences would you accept? Name the issue precisely: warmer color, sharper edges, smoother face or a conspicuous boundary. Approve the materials as they look during that comparison.

## Choose the smallest scope that delivers an acceptable result

### Localized replacement

Ask whether replacing only damaged or unsuitable units can preserve the rest of the driveway. Have the contractor confirm that limited work is feasible before deciding whether you accept the demonstrated variation. Which replacements would remain noticeable, especially in a prominent location? Identify the units to retain and the units to replace, then review the proposed arrangement. Record the accepted contrast beside each affected area.

### Deliberate contrast or a border

Consider a contrasting border or clearly separated added area when a convincing match is unavailable and you like the driveway you have. Ask whether that design change would involve more material or installation work than isolated replacement. Have the contractor show where the treatment starts and stops, how it relates to the existing pattern, and whether the physical transition works. Review the whole arrangement with the retained driveway visible beside it.

### A wider, defined section

Ask whether replacing a coherent section would put the transition at a boundary you prefer. Would that approach address clustered damage or reduce the visual interruption of scattered replacements? Have the contractor identify the boundary on site and explain what additional work it introduces. Ask how much existing material would be disturbed and what contrast would remain. Compare that scope with localized replacement before authorizing a wider area.

## Decide which existing units to retain

Ask which units are serviceable and which need replacement, then decide what appearance you will approve alongside them. If maintenance of the retained area is proposed, include that treated area in the sample review. Confirm whether any serviceable units will be kept as spares and where they will be stored after the work.

## Approve the scope before authorizing the order

Leave the sample review with a written decision that covers more than a color name:

- **The material:** record the identified product, dimensions and finish. Confirm availability with the supplier and specify whether any substitution requires your approval.
- **The reference:** identify the physical sample and the prepared existing area used for comparison. Keep photographs as supporting records of that review.
- **The boundaries:** mark retained units, replacement areas and any border or added section. Ask who confirms compatibility, levels and the condition of the supporting installation.
- **The work:** distinguish maintenance, material supply and installation. Record accepted visible differences, unresolved questions and who authorizes changes if the agreed material cannot be supplied.
