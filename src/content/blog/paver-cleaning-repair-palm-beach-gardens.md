## Start with what you can see

Walk the driveway and record what bothers you in plain language. A patch that looks darker, a joint that looks empty, and a paver that moves underfoot are different observations. None, by itself, tells you what is happening underneath.

Photograph the same area when it is dry and after natural rain, if you have the opportunity. Include a wider view so the contractor can locate the spot. Note whether it is new, recurring, or previously repaired. There is no need to create a puddle with a hose to prepare for a consultation.

Gather any old invoice, sealer name, product label photo, or record of previous work. If the product is unknown, say so. Describe a white film as a white film; its color alone is not enough to name the cause or choose a treatment.

## Agree on repairs before the final treatment

CMHA's [Cleaning, Sealing and Joint Sand Stabilization of Interlocking Concrete Pavement](https://www.masonryandhardscapes.org/resource/pav-tec-005/) recommends inspecting for cracked or broken units, treating specific stains before overall cleaning, and trying products in a small area according to their instructions. It also calls for protecting nearby vegetation and considering where cleaning fluids will drain, including low spots.

For your estimate, that means agreeing on what the contractor needs to inspect before approving the full visit. If a corner needs attention, have that area identified on a photo or sketch. The proposal should distinguish resetting existing pavers from supplying replacements and explain what happens if lifting them reveals additional work.

A small repair deserves its own estimate. It does not automatically justify replacing the driveway. A clean-and-seal price also needs to make clear whether uneven areas will be corrected. When replacement is recommended, the explanation should identify what makes a limited repair unsuitable for your property.

## Make the written scope specific

A price for cleaning can cover very different work from a price for cleaning, re-sanding, and sealing. The useful comparison starts with the same measured area and a clear description of each task.

The proposal should make these points easy to find:

- **Area included:** The driveway boundaries, borders, and any adjoining walk, with excluded portions marked so the crew and homeowner share the same plan.
- **Stain treatment:** The spots being evaluated, whether treatment is included, and what the cleaning trial suggests may remain visible.
- **Repairs:** The locations, whether existing pavers will be reused, and how any additional work will be priced and approved before it proceeds.
- **Joint work:** The areas to be re-sanded, the proposed joint material, and whether the price includes preparing those joints.
- **Sealing:** The product and intended finish, along with any preparation proposed for the existing surface treatment.
- **Exclusions:** Drainage construction, replacement units, or removal of an old coating listed separately when they are outside the quoted work.

CMHA treats joint sand stabilizers and sealers as distinct products whose functions can overlap.

This detail gives you a way to compare estimates without guessing what a short service label includes. It also leaves a record you can use at the final walkthrough: the agreed repair locations, treated area, finish, and any remaining items should all be identifiable.

## Set expectations for the finish and access

Evaluate a small sample for color, sheen, and remaining marks before approving the finish. Discuss how proposed replacement pavers will look alongside the existing ones.

CMHA advises turning off automatic sprinklers during cleaning, sealing, and drying.

Arrange who will manage that setting. Share your planned use date, and have the contractor explain the selected products' requirements for keeping cars and pets off the surface and returning it to use. Treat the date as a scheduling need, not a promised cure time.

For a gated property, check access and scheduling needs with your community contact. The proposal should also identify who moves vehicles and furniture, how access to the house will work, and who confirms that the driveway is ready for use.
