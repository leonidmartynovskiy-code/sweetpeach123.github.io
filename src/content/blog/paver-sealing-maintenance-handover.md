## Set the handover expectation before approving work

Ask the contractor to describe the proposed handover while you are reviewing the scope. A promise to explain maintenance later leaves you unable to judge what information will accompany the finished service.

Your file should answer these questions:

- **Product:** What exact product was applied, including the variant or finish? Which documents describe its ongoing care?
- **Treated area:** Which named surfaces received treatment? Where do treated areas end, and what was excluded?
- **Application date:** When was each area treated? Does the final record reflect any change from the planned work?
- **Maintenance instructions:** What guidance covers subsequent cleaning, furniture use, spills and requests for further assessment?
- **Contact:** Who should receive a care question, and what information should accompany it?

Keep proposed details distinct from the final record. If a product or work area changes, ask for the handover to be updated. An early estimate should not remain your only account of what was applied.

## Keep the instructions you can actually use

CMHA's [Cleaning, Sealing and Joint Sand Stabilization of Interlocking Concrete Pavement](https://www.cmha.org/resource/pav-tec-005/) recommends products intended for concrete pavers and careful adherence to label directions and warnings. Its guidance concerns concrete; it should not be transferred to porcelain. Product identification is therefore essential to a useful care file.

Save the supplied instructions as readable files or paper copies, alongside any contractor clarification. A product name alone is less useful when someone needs to decide how to clean a spill. Keep the relevant care document accessible to the person who will maintain the area, rather than buried among unrelated renovation emails.

Ask the contractor to identify which document answers which question. A product brochure, an invoice and a maintenance sheet serve different purposes. If verbal advice appears to conflict with the supplied written guidance, request clarification in writing before acting on it.

## Pass the care plan to the people using the space

Keep the contractor's guidance for subsequent cleaning together: the permitted methods, any stated restrictions and the contact for an unfamiliar situation. Before a returning cleaning contractor proposes a different treatment, give them that information for review. Do not substitute a general pressure setting or cleaning mixture for the instructions supplied for the actual surface and product.

Furniture use deserves a clear entry too. Ask what precautions apply when moving or placing furniture, and keep any continuing restrictions visible. If the household later changes the furniture or adds a heavy planter, send the installer the proposed location and use for clarification rather than assuming the original advice answers the new situation.

## Make stain questions easy to assess

Ask at handover what to do when a spill occurs and whom to contact if the supplied instructions do not address it. Keep that response guidance where it can be found quickly. A household member should not need to search old messages to find the installer while deciding what action is appropriate.

When requesting a spot assessment, describe what you know: the location, when you first noticed the mark and any spill or activity you actually witnessed. If the cause is unknown, say so. Include what has already been done to the area, with the name of any cleaning product used.

Send an overview that locates the concern and a close view that shows it. Add the treatment record so the recipient can connect the question to the correct surface. Ask what action is appropriate and whether the contractor needs to inspect it before you attempt further treatment.

## Record changes without prescribing the repair

CMHA's [Operation and Maintenance Guide for Interlocking Concrete Pavement](https://www.cmha.org/resource/pav-tec-006/) recommends inspections and provides a record template. It distinguishes visual concerns from distress affecting structural performance. That distinction matters when describing a change: appearance alone does not identify the work needed.

Keep dated site photos and brief maintenance observations together. Record new spots, loose joint material or movement for assessment. Note the location and what changed, without assigning a cause.

For each maintenance visit, note who attended, what they reported doing and any follow-up instructions. Save later correspondence beside the original handover, so a new photograph is not separated from the response it received. An unresolved question should remain easy to find until you have an answer.

## Separate care instructions from written coverage

A handover should not be read as a warranty promise. If you receive a separate written warranty or service agreement, retain its actual terms: who provides it, what it covers, its exclusions and the procedure for raising a concern. Do not add assumptions about free visits, stain removal or future treatments.

Keep those terms with the invoice and care contact. When a question arises, ask whether the proposed response falls within an existing agreement or requires separately approved work.
